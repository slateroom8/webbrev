The three Brévant selections

Vancouver

Between the mountains and the Pacific, three kitchens that could belong to no other city.

AnnaLena

Kitsilano

AnnaLena makes a serious case for not taking the room too seriously. Michael Robbins and Jeff Parr run a Kitsilano restaurant where Lego sculptures and nostalgic bric-a-brac pop against moody navy walls, and the mischief carries onto the plate — a multi-course tasting that repackages familiar flavours in unfamiliar ways, like an elk-tartare riff on a cheeseburger. Since 2020 Robbins has committed fully to the tasting format, all of it grounded in seasonal British Columbia ingredients and a refined technique the playfulness can disguise. It is fine dining without the starch: warm, inventive, and confident enough to be funny, which is far harder to pull off than solemnity.

Kissa Tanto

Chinatown

Kissa Tanto sits up a flight of stairs in Chinatown, in a room modelled on the jazz cafés of 1960s Tokyo — moody, dim, hung with antique panels and old photographs. The conceit could be a gimmick; instead it frames one of the city's most distinctive kitchens. Joël Watanabe and Tannis Ling built the place around the unlikely marriage of Japanese and Italian cooking, and it works: hand-cut tajarin dressed with miso-cured egg yolk, crudo alongside pasta, a tiramisu that turns eastward. Nearly a decade on, the food still surprises without straining for it. It is a restaurant to linger in, which is precisely what the setting was designed to make you do.

Sumibiyaki Arashi

Mount Pleasant

Sumibiyaki Arashi raised the bar for yakitori in Vancouver, and did it from a fourteen-seat counter in Mount Pleasant. Pete Ho grills over binchotan charcoal, drawing clean, deep flavour from heritage-breed chicken through careful butchery, a tare maintained over decades, and a feel for exactly when each skewer is done. The omakase is anchored in those skewers but moves beyond them — a yuzu-scented crab chawanmushi, delicately fried tofu, seasonal fish threaded through the progression. Blending Ho's Taiwanese background with Japanese technique, it treats a humble form with the seriousness usually reserved for sushi. The result is uncomplicated on the surface and quietly profound underneath, which is the whole art of it.
