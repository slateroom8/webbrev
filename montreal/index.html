The three Brévant selections

Montréal

A city that treats a great meal as a birthright, distilled here to three distinctive tables.

Mon Lapin

Little Italy

Mon Lapin has spent years in the narrow, wonderful space between neighbourhood wine bar and destination, never straining to be either. In a bare-tabled Little Italy room, co-chefs Marc-Olivier Frappier and Jessica Noël send out small plates that read simply on the menu — an ingredient, a producer — then surprise, like leeks tinned as if they were mussels or a pressed-scallop sandwich that has become a signature. Vanya Filipovic's cellar is the other half of the argument: a deep, idiosyncratic list of low-intervention wines chosen to keep pace with a menu that changes constantly. The energy is loose and the cooking is not, and that tension is the whole appeal.

Beba

Verdun

Brothers Ari and Pablo Schor named Beba for their grandmother, and the restaurant keeps that domestic warmth even as the cooking reaches well past it. Tucked onto a residential Verdun corner, the small room draws on the Spanish and Italian threads that run through Argentine food, filtered through Ari's years in serious Montreal kitchens. The menu is short — a handful of plates that might run from marinated mackerel on sesame bread to a generous pasta — bold in flavour, unfussy in presentation, and poured alongside a smart natural-wine list. It is the kind of place Montreal does better than almost anywhere: casual on its face, quietly accomplished underneath, and genuinely a neighbourhood's own.

Sushi Nishinokaze

Mile End

Sushi Nishinokaze brings a rigour to Montreal the city had no real equivalent for. Vincent Gee trained in the Edomae tradition and now runs an eight-seat counter in Mile End, where the omakase turns on things most diners never think about: pesticide-free rice seasoned with artisanal red vinegar, its temperature matched to the fat of each fish; skilful curing and aging; wild seafood flown in several times a week alongside the best Canadian catch. The room doubles as a living gallery of ceramics spanning millennia, each piece in use rather than on display. It is exacting, unhurried and complete — a restaurant for anyone who wants to watch a craft performed at its edge.
